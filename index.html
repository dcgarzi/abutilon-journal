Flower form — the aperture ladder

The single most diagnostic trait in Abutilon: how open the flower is, from sealed tube to flat dish, ordered as a ladder built from Monterey Bay Nursery's cultivar vocabulary. The pinched waist gets its own step because it marks the megapotamicum line. Some plants span a range flower-to-flower ('Twister' runs partially-flared to fully flat) — record a range if you see one. Form is petal silhouette only; calyx and stamens are scored separately.

1 · Tubular / barely open"Very narrow, barely opening." Exemplar: 'Souvenir de Bonn'.
2 · Waisted lanternInflated body constricts at the waist, then a skirt flares below. Exemplars: 'Challo', 'Sunset', 'Victory'. Megapotamicum-line marker.
3 · Bell-shapedThe classic hanging bell; length ≈ width, mouth half-open. Exemplar: 'Sangria'. Your mother tree.
4 · Partially flaredMouth opens noticeably; sides still curve inward. Exemplar: 'Frieda'.
5 · Globe / rounded"More or less globe shaped" — petals arc back inward, ball-like. Exemplar: 'Rosalie'.
6 · Widely flaredPetals flare wide, approaching horizontal. Exemplar: 'Pink Parasol'.
7 · Flat / fully open"Very wide, very open" — petals essentially horizontal. Exemplar: 'Victor Reiter'.

Step 0 is "not yet bloomed." Drawings locked from our shape-review session. Cultivar links open Monterey Bay Nursery's reference photos (internet required); journal card icons render these same seven shapes in each plant's recorded petal, calyx, and stamen colors.

Calyx — score shape, color, and size as separate traits

These three axes vary independently and recombine in seedlings: a calyx can be green and flared, red and tiny, inflated and flushed. The shapes below are drawn colorless on purpose — record color and size alongside. (Ghost petals shown for scale.)

Shape: tight / claspingSepals hug the petal base, tips pointing down along it — your mother's shape (dusky maroon, medium-sized).
Shape: flared / reflexedSides sweep outward, sepal tips curling back.
Shape: inflated / ribbedBalloon with keeled ribs, pinching in where the petals emerge (straight megapotamicum).
Color axis: green → flushed → dusky maroon (your mother's) → bright red — independent of shape.  ·  Size axis: small (petals dwarf it) ↔ large/showy (rivals the petals) — hers is medium. Also worth scoring: bud color — her fat dusky-maroon buds are an early anthocyanin tell. The Journal form records these separately.

Leaves

"Flowering maple" = maple-like leaves, but lobing varies enormously and is one of the easiest traits to score on seedlings before first bloom. Score four separate axes: primary vein count (veins radiating from the base — 3 / 5 / 7), lobe count, sinus depth, and margin. Each primary vein marks a potential lobe, so lobes ≤ veins — but lobes don't have to develop: your mother is 5-veined with only 3 pronounced lobes (the basal pair barely express). Vein count stays constant across a plant while lobing varies with leaf age and position, so veins are the more reliable count for comparing seedlings.

Unlobed (ovate–cordate)Heart-shaped base, tapering drip tip, serrate edge, no lobes. Common in megapotamicum lines.
3-lobedCentral lobe + two side lobes, palmate veins to each tip. Your mother's count — hers has a long pointed center lobe with short side lobes, medium sinus. Sinus depth is its own axis.
5-lobed (maple-like)Five pointed lobes. Strong hybridum trait — not your mother's, so a 5-lobed seedling is worth noting.
Sinus depth — separate axisSame 3 lobes as the tile above, but a shallow sinus. Score shallow / medium / deep for any lobed leaf — it inherits independently of lobe count.
Margin: serrateSharp forward-pointing teeth (most Abutilon). Crenate = rounded teeth; note tooth size.
Variegation (mottled)⚠ Yellow mosaic mottling in Abutilon is usually caused by Abutilon mosaic virus, spread by grafting/whitefly — NOT inherited through seed. Don't score it as genetic.
PubescenceDowny hairs on stems/leaf undersides. Score: smooth / soft-hairy / felty. Easily checked on young seedlings.

Cultivating from seed

Abutilon is in the mallow family, and mallow seeds have a hard, water-resistant seed coat — this is the #1 reason they sit in soil doing nothing. The fix is mechanical: scarify, then soak. Untreated seed can take months or never sprout; treated seed typically germinates in 9–21 days.

1Harvest ripe seedLet pods (schizocarps) dry brown and papery on the plant, then shake seeds out. Seed from green pods is immature and won't sprout. Fresh seed germinates far better than stored.
2Cut-test a sampleSacrifice 3–5 seeds: slice in half. Firm, pale interior = viable. Hollow, dusty, or shriveled = dead — common in open-pollinated hybrid seed. If most are hollow, no technique will save the batch; collect fresher pods.
3ScarifyRub each seed on fine sandpaper (or nick with a nail clipper) until you just see a pale spot of the interior. You're breaching the waterproof coat, not carving. This step alone often doubles germination speed and count.
4Soak overnightDrop scarified seeds in hand-hot water (~120–140°F / 50–60°C to start) and leave 12–24 hours as it cools. Viable scarified seeds usually swell visibly. Sow immediately after — don't let them dry out again.
5Sow shallow¼ inch (5 mm) deep, no deeper, in moist sterile seed-starting mix (not garden soil). Water once with a fine rose, then keep evenly moist, never soggy. Too deep is a classic silent failure.
6Warmth + humidity70–80°F (21–27°C) is the trigger — cool rooms are why August-sown seed indoors sulks. A seedling heat mat under the tray plus a clear dome or bag on top is the reliable combo. Bright light, not hot direct sun.
7Sprout → prick outExpect sprouts in 9–21 days. Vent the dome as soon as they're up (trapped humid air = damping-off). Pot on individually once the first true (lobed) leaves appear. Log each one in the Journal tab that day.

Propagating from cuttings

Cuttings clone the parent exactly — the only way to duplicate your mother tree or a favorite seedling, since seed shuffles the genes. Abutilon roots readily if the cutting is the right age of wood: soft, green, flexible spring–summer growth. The #1 failure is using old, brown, woody stems (which is most of what a 6ft scraggly tree offers at eye level) or flowering shoots.

1Choose the right shootSoft, green, pencil-thin tip growth, 4–6″, no flowers or buds (pinch them off or pick another shoot — flowers steal the energy roots need). Brown, stiff wood = wrong. Take cuttings in the morning, spring through late summer.
2Prep the cuttingCut just below a node (roots form there). Strip all lower leaves — bare nodes go in the mix. Keep only the top 2 leaves; slice large ones in half crosswise to cut water loss. Work fast; don't let it wilt.
3Hormone dipMoisten the bottom inch, dip in rooting hormone powder or gel (IBA ~0.3%), tap off excess. Optional for many plants; for Abutilon it meaningfully raises the strike rate and speed. Replace hormone that's more than ~2 years old — it degrades.
4Stick in gritty mix50/50 perlite + seed-starting mix (or perlite + vermiculite). Not potting soil — it holds too much water and rots the stem, the classic cause of "no roots, black mushy base." Insert 1.5″ deep (2 nodes buried), firm gently, water once.
5Humidity tentClear bag propped on sticks (not touching leaves) or a dome. Bright indirect light — direct sun cooks a tented cutting in an hour. 70–75°F; bottom heat helps here too. Flip the bag daily to shed condensation and give 5 min of air.
6Wait — don't tugRoots take 3–6 weeks. Pulling to check snaps the fragile new roots — the sign to watch for instead is fresh new leaf growth. Give it another week after that, then pot on and harden off over several days before full sun.

For your situation specifically: seeds — cut-test your stash first; if they're hollow, that's the whole answer, and the fix is harvesting fresher pods off the tree (late summer/fall pods drying now are ideal). If they're firm, scarify + overnight soak + heat mat will almost certainly break the logjam. Cuttings — your 6ft scraggly tree is mostly old wood; hard-prune a branch or two, wait 3–4 weeks for soft regrowth, and take those shoots with the perlite + tent + no-flowers protocol. Your breeding population — the cross-reference table

Every seedling under your tree has 'Red Emperor' as its seed parent. The pollen parent is any flowering maple within bee range — a few hundred yards, including varieties you've never seen. So don't read fathers as specific plants; read them as lineage archetypes. Garden abutilons fall into two broad lines whose structural traits travel together, while flower color is an independent axis any donor of either line can carry. To read a seedling: go trait by trait and see which column each structural trait points to. Traits won't all point the same way — that's recombination, and it's the interesting part.

Why one red tree throws so much variety

'Red Emperor' is itself a complex hybrid (A. × hybridum), which means she is heterozygous at many genes — carrying hidden alleles her own flowers never show. So even selfed seed segregates: mostly orange-reds like her, but deeper crimsons, paler apricots, pinks, and the occasional yellow are expected without any foreign pollen at all. A yellow seedling is your proof of what she carries:

R (from mother)r (from mother)
r (yellow father)Rr — red layer ON → red/orangerr — red layer OFF → yellow
r (yellow father)Rr — red layer ON → red/orangerr — red layer OFF → yellow

If she's a carrier (Rr), red × yellow gives ≈ half pigmented, half yellow. If she's RR, zero yellows — ever. Your running tally in the Journal is the experiment: once you've logged ~10 seedlings from yellow-father crosses, the ratio answers her genotype. Dose genes on top of this on/off switch smear the pigmented half into crimson-through-apricot, which is why you get a spectrum rather than two neat piles.

Leveling up: controlled crosses

Open-pollinated seed tells you the mother; the decoder above only estimates the father. To know both: pick a mother bud about to open, remove petals and anthers before pollen sheds (emasculation), brush on your chosen father's pollen, bag the flower in mesh, and tag it. Then the seedling's traits become real data instead of detective work. Selfing an interesting F1 (to get an F2) is where hidden recessives segregate out in force — that's where the surprises live.

Naming note: botanists moved the ornamental flowering maples from Abutilon to Callianthe. Horticulture still says Abutilon; either is correct in your records.
